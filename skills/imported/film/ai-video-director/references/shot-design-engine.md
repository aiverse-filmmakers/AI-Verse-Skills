## 1. Scope

This file derives shot design from the script. It does not rewrite the story or decide whether a video model will ultimately succeed.

The hierarchy in this version is:

```text
Understand script and character action
→ Design blocking and the shot sequence
→ Use the full cinematography vocabulary to design shot function, size, position, optics, focus, editing, and compound movement
→ Apply this version's production contract for spatial and physical continuity
→ Deliver the requested shot plan and/or video prompt
```

Do not give the user analysis processes, scoring tables, or internal ledgers. Formal output is governed by `production-contract.md`.

## 2. Develop the director's understanding from the script

Read the entire scene first. Do not split shots mechanically by number of sentences, lines, or actions. Internally retell in natural language:

> Who is in what situation, what action do they take toward what goal, who or what resists them, what action or reaction changes the situation, and what do the relationships, choices, or result become?

Understand at least:

- Events, characters, dialogue, places, times, and action results explicitly established by the script.
- What each character actually wants now and whether surface action matches the real goal.
- How conflict happens through specific action, space, objects, pauses, or reactions.
- What objective, relationship, information, emotion, choice, or cost genuinely changes from scene start to finish.
- Which interpretations come from the text and which are director inferences.

Do not turn these into a fill-in form; together they form one complete understanding. Ask one decisive question only when two interpretations would produce materially different shot plans and the text cannot settle them.

## 3. Turn understanding into blocking

First decide how characters accomplish the story in space, then how the camera watches.

1. Fix world positions of scene structure, routes, doors, windows, furniture, characters, and props.
2. State what each person currently approaches, avoids, controls, hides, protects, or takes.
3. Find actions that actually change the situation, such as entering, leaving, approaching, yielding, closing a door, handing over, taking, stopping, or turning.
4. Change at least one observable relationship through the action: distance, orientation, center, depth, occlusion, exit, affiliation, prop ownership, or freedom of action.
5. Background characters move only when the story world requires them, with direction, purpose, and sustained action. Do not create pointless activity merely for richness.

Blocking is not background to camera motion. Rehearse them together: characters trigger the camera, the camera reorganizes relationships, and the ending composition shows the new situation after the action.

### 3.1 Multi-space action and user-specified shot references

- A shot should have one clear primary space and viewing task. When characters perform different actions in separate rooms or outdoors, do not force them into a doorway, corridor spying view, or cross-room wide shot merely to show both simultaneously.
- Different spaces have independent visual centers. First establish the character-environment relationship where the action actually occurs, then enter the next space by cutting, sound bridge, action match, or an existing passage. Spatial separation is not new story.
- When the user explicitly assigns a scene or shot image to a scene or first shot, its camera position, height, perspective, horizon, screen left and right, main furniture proportions, and visible boundaries become hard constraints for that shot. Do not replace them with cat-height, tracking, outside-door, reverse, overhead, or different establishing angles without authorization.
- The reference locks only the shot explicitly assigned to it; it does not automatically constrain other shots. Recalculate later camera changes from the same world, without retroactively changing the locked composition.
- If a character acts in an off-screen space in the reference, keep them completely out of that shot and show them later where appropriate. Do not put them at a door, edge, or background merely to prove they are present.
- A person or animal must enter through a door, passage, space behind an occlusion, or established off-screen entrance that actually exists in the reference. If the edge is a solid wall, TV cabinet, sofa, or has no passable entry, do not write an entrance from that side. Place the subject on a visible floor anchor in the first frame or use an earlier shot with a real entrance.

## 4. Design the shot sequence first

Establish the visual structure before filling the shot table.

### 4.1 Choose the organization

- **Continuous long take**: action, travel through space, and relationship changes can unfold along one readable path.
- **Cut coverage**: compare, emphasize a reaction, omit time, change the information focus, or create strong rhythm.
- **Mixed structure**: sustained blocking carries the process and cuts land on key actions, reactions, or results.

Choose how the script action can work most clearly and powerfully, not a preset preference for long takes or fast cuts.

### 4.2 Relationships between shots

Address the relevant changes across the sequence:

- Viewing distance: transitions among environmental scale, character relations, physical action, expression, and detail.
- Camera relationships: front, side, rear-side, axis side, height, and tilt as the situation changes.
- Spatial depth: narrative use of foreground, middle, background, occlusion, and passageways.
- Camera-subject relation: fixed observation, leading, following, parallel, opposing, orbiting, or subject handoff.
- Motion rhythm: contrast among stillness, start, steady speed, acceleration, deceleration, pause, abrupt stop, and cuts.
- Handoff: action, eyeline, sound, composition, direction, or story outcome carrying one shot into the next.

Richness comes from relationships changing with the story, not shot count, terminology count, or forced cycling of shot sizes.

## 5. Combining shot size and camera position

Shot size says how much needs to be seen now; it does not directly equal an emotion. Assess together:

- Frame needed to read the action scale and spatial route.
- Number of people, distance, and occlusion needed to read relationships.
- Whether performance changes occur in body, hand, face, or environmental consequences.
- Whether the relationship with surrounding shots should progress, hold, or jump.
- Whether camera distance, focal length, depth of field, and background work together.

Do not use a fixed wide-to-medium-to-close-up staircase. Jumping from a wide shot to a detail, suddenly releasing a close shot to a wide, or changing relationships through blocking and focus within the same size are allowed when the scene supports them.

Record camera direction, height, pitch, axis side, and viewpoint distance. Do not conflate low camera, upward angle, and wide lens; do not mechanically equate angle with power.

For framing first assess visual weight and head, look, and movement room, then choose geometry and depth. Establish the axis from the current relationship or action before judging camera side. See `references/framing-and-axis.md`. Correct coordinate projection alone does not prove cut direction is continuous. Character swaps, a moving axis, or deliberate axis crossing need an explicit reestablishment process, not merely a “maintain axis” label.

## 6. Construct compound camera moves

Design a compound move as one continuous shot event:

```text
Starting camera position and frame
→ In-frame character, object, or sound trigger
→ First path stage and subject relationship
→ Pause, occlusion, focus, or subject handoff
→ Second path stage and changes in height, orientation, or distance
→ Speed curve and movement quality changes
→ Ending frame, action result, and next-shot interface
```

Each stage may combine:

- Camera-center tracking, rising, lowering, advancing, retreating, or diagonal movement.
- Orientation changes such as pan, tilt, and roll.
- Straight, diagonal, curved, orbiting, or multistage paths.
- Fixed, leading, following, parallel, opposing, orbiting, relay, or independent reveal.
- Entrances, exits, crossed positions, reveal after occlusion, or movement through depth.
- Focus handoff between characters, action, objects, reflections, or background events.
- Stillness, gradual start, acceleration, deceleration, pause, or abrupt stop.
- Stable, floating, weighted handheld, mechanical precision, or other physical motion quality.

Each stage must have a distinct visible function. An orbit that changes no profile, background, occlusion, or relationship is merely circling. A rise that changes neither spatial scale nor information is showmanship. A push without a performance or action beat is just an emotion label.

Complex shots may include stillness and pauses. If one continuous path makes action, relationships, or space hard to read, redesign as a mixed shot sequence; do not stop after deciding complexity is unsuitable.

### 6.1 Staged rehearsal and time density

First map a physically passable camera path, then arrange orientation, focus, and character handoffs. Describe camera-center movement, pan and tilt, roll, and zoom separately; “push-rise-pan” alone is insufficient. Each handoff must specify the condition by which the former object leaves visual prominence, where the next appears, and which world anchor remains recognizable. Occlusion can hide a stitch but does not make passing through walls or teleportation valid.

Allocate shot time to observable phases: recognize the initial state, unfold action, show key contact or discovery, and hold a reaction or result. Concurrent camera and action can share time; dependent steps such as “see, reach, grasp, aim, install, confirm” cannot overlap into one instantaneous event. For fine hand work, if camera acceleration, continuous focus pulling, or a rise hides contact, postpone the handoff, hold a stable composition through the action, or adjust unlocked shot organization.

Do not impose a uniform duration per phase or treat shot count as richness. Estimate from path length, subject speed, action precision, and audience recognition. If a key period works only at abnormal speed, simplify competing camera tasks first without deleting the result or swallowing dialogue. If user-locked action and duration remain incompatible, report the conflict; do not extend total duration without authorization or hide it with a slow-motion label.

Rehearse stillness and pauses too: how do occlusion, distance, or empty space change inside the frame, how long must the audience watch, and which visible beat triggers the cut? Fixed cameras do not require irrelevant constant micro-actions. Intentional stillness, held breath, and refusal to react are valid.

### Short example

Story change: a person in power reaches for evidence, but it starts playing proof of their wrongdoing; investigators then appear at the door, reversing control.

Shot design: begin in a two-person medium shot with the powerful person foregrounded and a subordinate near the door in the background. As the person reaches, the camera moves laterally toward the table; playback causes the first abrupt stop. The subordinate moves aside, and the camera uses their body as an occlusion to arc to the other side of the table and rise slightly, handing focus from evidence to arrivals at the door. When the powerful person rises, the camera no longer follows, leaving them trapped in depth between table corner and arrivals. The ending frame turns the former foreground controller into someone enclosed by space. Complexity arises from continuous reversals of relationship and space, not a pile of camera terms.

## 7. Exploration and production

- **Exploration**: when a meaningful creative decision benefits from comparison, internally consider genuinely different shot sequences, for example in blocking structure, continuous versus cut organization, shot-size curve, or camera path. Show candidates only when the user needs to choose.
- **Production**: follow the confirmed direction and deliver one completed design. Do not change shot order, dialogue, positions, rhythm, or script facts merely to demonstrate creativity.

Two plans are not different merely because a push becomes an orbit or medium becomes close. If blocking, story endpoint, and shot relationships remain the same, they are still one plan.

## 8. Internal viewpoint checks

After a shot draft, internally check:

- Can the visual priority be read naturally from action, frame, focus, and sound?
- Has information needed for the story accidentally been disclosed too early or obscured?
- Does the camera-character relationship remain coherent after cuts and handoffs?
- Does the shot endpoint land accurately on the scene's changed state?

These are checks, not the creative starting point. Do not require user forms, add them as formal storyboard fields, or substitute them for whole-script understanding.

## 9. Failure modes and internal acceptance

### Failure modes

- Mapping dramatic adjectives directly to fixed moves: tension equals handheld, sadness equals slow push, power equals low angle.
- Choosing moves first and inventing story reasons later.
- Moving every shot without stillness/motion, speed, scale, and cut contrast.
- Cycling shot sizes on a fixed staircase without changes in character relationship or action scale.
- A compound move without in-frame triggers, staged functions, or ending composition.
- Changing camera-move names while blocking, paths, relationships, and story endpoints remain identical.
- Adding characters, actions, objects, story, or dialogue without authorization to enrich shots.
- Correct spatial and lighting continuity but no directing judgment or visual rhythm across the sequence.

### Internal acceptance

1. Can one natural sentence explain what actually changes in this scene?
2. Does the design arise from character action, relationships, space, and result rather than abstract emotional labels?
3. Is there a readable shot sequence with meaningful changes in shot size, position, depth, motion, or editing?
4. Does every compound-move stage have an in-frame trigger, distinct purpose, and clear endpoint?
5. Would removing or replacing a shot lose action clarity, relationship change, story landing, or rhythmic structure?
6. Does it fully pass the current `production-contract.md` continuity and output acceptance?

## 10. Professional theory

This version does not assemble shots from a camera-movement word list. It links these professional layers into a causal chain:

- **Dramatic action and scene change**: understand scenes through character goals, resistance, actions, and changes in goals, relationships, information, emotion, choices, and cost; complete decision rules are in this section.
- **Blocking**: translate character goals and relationship changes into positions, movement, entrances, exits, occlusion, revelation, depth, and spatial boundaries; see this file's blocking and space sections.
- **Shot behavior**: combine camera direction, height, tilt, shot size, optics, movement path, camera-subject relationship, movement quality, time treatment, and continuity into complete camera behavior; see this file and the cinematography engine.
- **Shot size and coverage**: shot size records the subject's scale in frame; establishing, reaction, insert, and master are shot functions, not a list of sizes.
- **Camera kinematics**: distinguish camera-center displacement from orientation changes and construct compound motion from path geometry, subject relationships, speed curves, and end states.
- **Editing relationships**: organize handoffs through continuity, action, sound, reaction, ellipsis, and rhythm, without hiding action paths or story causality behind fast cuts.

These professional layers are fully contained in the current runtime package. They supply observable causal grounds, not a mechanical “story type equals shot” formula. Final shots must be derived jointly from the current script, performance, spatial conditions, and overall sequence rhythm.
