# Framing, Camera Position, and Axis Decisions

## 1. Purpose and entry point

This file turns understanding of the script into frame organization and spatial continuity across shots. Use it with this package's `shot-design-engine.md`, `cinematography-design-engine.md`, and `production-contract.md`; it does not require an external knowledge base.

First establish the scene's action, character objectives, resistance, and change in situation, and design the complete shot sequence. Once a draft exists, internally check the attention order, readability of relationships, and disclosure of information in the actual compositions. Do not turn these questions into a preliminary form the user must fill out.

Rule of thirds, centered framing, symmetry, diagonals, and negative space are optional methods. None is required in every scene or has a one-to-one relationship with sadness, power, or tension. Fixed framing, complex movement, or cut coverage may each win on actual viewing effect.

User-locked world facts, reference-image purposes, camera positions, shot structure, and prohibitions take priority. Refine only unlocked cinematography; do not add story, characters, props, environmental events, or delivery layers on this basis.

## 2. Five levels of composition judgment

### 2.1 Visual hierarchy and weight

- After the draft exists, check the main focus of attention and any necessary shifts against the actual shot, including the visible change that triggers a shift. Do not force a second or hidden subject into a shot with one subject or no concealed information.
- Visual weight depends on brightness, color contrast, size, sharpness, faces, text, and movement. A small bright or moving object can steal attention.
- Check whether background, reflections, doorways, lights, and subtitle areas compete with the main action. Adjust framing, occlusion, focus, or blocking within the allowed scope; do not invent reasons to delete story information.
- Deliberately dispersed attention, delayed discovery, or concealment can work, but explain the narrative purpose and when the audience regains necessary information.

### 2.2 Frame margins

- **Headroom**: choose the space above the head based on shot size, pose, and frame pressure. Do not maintain an identical gap mechanically or mistake an intentional tight facial crop for an error.
- **Look room**: first determine the object or off-screen area actually being watched, then choose space on the facing side. Reverse lead room may express obstruction or isolation but must not accidentally change the object of attention.
- **Movement room**: preserve a readable route ahead of motion or deliberately bring the subject near the edge to create pressure. Confirm important contact, turns, and results are not unintentionally cropped.
- **Edge tension**: check limbs, joints, props, door edges, and partial visibility. An intentional crop must serve the framing task and not hide what is necessary to understand the action.
- Margins change with character action and camera path. Checking only the first frame does not prove the entire shot is readable.

### 2.3 Geometric organization

Choose and compare methods for the current scene problem rather than merely labeling an image:

| Method | Viewing problem it can solve | Misuse to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Thirds and offset | Place subject, eyeline target, or action space together | Crowding out key action to hit a grid; fixing everyone in place |
| Center and symmetry | Focus attention, establish order, show opposing relationships or broken order | Neat art direction with no change in dramatic relationship |
| Diagonals and leading lines | Organize action direction, conflict path, or attention handoff | Lines pointing to irrelevant objects; confusing scene diagonals with camera roll |
| Frame within frame and occlusion | Restrict the view, express isolation, control order of revelation | Decorative framing that hides necessary performance |
| Negative space and imbalance | Leave a readable place for absence, an unrevealed target, or a broken relationship | Empty space without narrative purpose; random frame tilt |

One method can produce different emotions, and one narrative task can be solved in multiple ways. Describe the chosen subject positions, boundaries, relationships, and changes without imposing fixed seconds, focal lengths, or movement templates.

### 2.4 Depth organization

First assign information to foreground, middle ground, and background; then check subject scale, separation, occlusion, silhouettes, pathways, and planes of focus. Multiple layers being present does not mean the audience can understand them simultaneously.

As people or camera move, check at each stage who occludes whom, who is revealed, who holds attention, and how the change expresses relationships. Deep focus, shallow depth, focus pulls, and enlarged foregrounds are options, not mandatory combinations.

### 2.5 Aspect ratio and viewing boundaries

Confirm final aspect ratio, essential action area, subtitle or interface regions, and required cropping. A landscape-to-portrait change requires reorganizing relationships; do not assume a center crop will work.

Judge whether evidence is recognizable at the expected display size and viewing duration. “Close-up,” “high resolution,” or a focal length cannot replace actual framing boundaries. If the destination or safe area is unknown, leave it unknown rather than invent precise values.

## 3. Camera position, orientation, and roll

Record separately the camera center's world position, direction relative to the subject, height, optical-axis pitch, horizontal orientation, and roll around the optical axis. Then choose viewpoint ownership, shot size, focal length, frame boundaries, and focused subject.

- Tracking, rising, advancing, and retreating change the camera center; pan, tilt, and roll change orientation or attitude. They may combine but must be described separately.
- A low camera is not automatically an upward angle, nor a high camera automatically downward. A tilted horizon does not prove the scene or person is actually falling over.
- Movement needs a start, staged path, relationship to subjects, orientation changes, speed, trigger, and endpoint. Paths may pause; not every shot needs movement.
- Check composition at the start, relationship changes, key actions, and end of a moving shot. Arcs, orbits, and handoffs also require checking for axis crossing and impassable space along the way.

## 4. Establish the current effective axis

The axis supports the audience's continuous understanding of relationships, eyelines, and action direction. A composition's centerline or architectural symmetry line is not the same as the 180-degree action axis.

1. Identify the relationship that most needs to be clear now: dialogue or confrontation between two people, person and target, chase direction, or action path. Establish the main axis from explicit positions or movement, not an invented line through frame center.
2. State who or what defines the axis or action route and its current effective stage. Inherit an established camera side; otherwise choose one based on performance, space, and information.
3. Determine camera side and permitted camera area in world coordinates. Continuous coverage normally remains on the same side until visible action or an explicit transition establishes a new relationship.
4. Record key characters' world orientations, eyeline targets, and movement directions, then derive screen left and right, eyelines, and entrances and exits for each shot.
5. Do not invent a two-person relationship axis for an empty scene. Still check established geography, moving subjects or paths, lighting, and interfaces with adjoining shots; mark the relationship axis not applicable when appropriate.

For three or more characters, ensembles, or simultaneous actions, choose the primary relationship axis for the current viewing task and identify secondary relationships that must be preserved. Do not force all relationships onto one invented axis or silently change the primary axis at every cut.

Choose the main axis based on who is acting or exchanging information with whom. A new main subject does not automatically erase the old geography. If needed, hand off the axis with readable shared framing, blocking, or a new establishing shot.

## 5. Cuts and axis updates

Before cutting, compare the previous exit state with the next entry state: which side each camera occupies, who looks at whom, where movement goes, whose shoulder is in the foreground, and whether key landmarks remain recognizable.

Conventional two-person same-axis shot/reverse-shot coverage should preserve intelligible reciprocal eyelines. “One looks left and one looks right” alone is insufficient: also check height, target position, and specific camera positions. Derive POV, on-axis, overhead, and multi-subject scenes from their actual geometry.

Blocking can rotate or replace the axis. First show the movement, turn, or target shift that changes the relationship, then update the axis and allowed camera side from the new positions. Do not merely change an internal axis label while characters swap places without cause in the edit.

When the main axis changes continuously with action, inspect the camera's relative side throughout the stage. Even a stationary camera can change sides if people move around one another; checking the camera path alone is insufficient.

**Correct world coordinates and projection do not guarantee editorial axis continuity.** A north-to-south camera change may correctly reverse screen left and right, but if the shots are adjacent, separately assess whether the audience understands the side change and whether locked continuity requirements are met.

## 6. Deliberate side changes and disorientation

- Make a side change clear by showing the movement around the axis in a continuous shot, or by using blocking that reveals the relationship change, an on-axis view, or a readable establishing shot to reestablish direction.
- Whether a transition works depends on its actual content. A neutral insert, black frame, or occlusion does not automatically reset the axis. Explain what visible relationship lets the audience understand the new direction.
- A continuous orbit is not automatic exemption; check that intervening occlusion, subject swaps, focus changes, and the endpoint preserve necessary directional anchors.
- Intentional disorientation is a separate directing choice. State its narrative purpose, scope, essential information to retain, and method for restoring direction. Do not call an unintended spatial error “artistic.”
- Do not use this section to release a user lock against crossing the axis, on camera side, or on existing shots. When a change is necessary, preserve the original design and follow existing handoff permissions.

## 7. Verifiable facts and limits of checks

Quantitative checks require explicit geometry: coordinate system and units, subject and target positions, camera position, current axis, and its effective stage. Checks of eyeline, framing, or motion additionally require orientation, aspect ratio or field of view, or path and timing as applicable. Do not guess coordinates from emotions, shot names, or missing data.

In a declared two-dimensional top-down coordinate system, the side of axis A→B occupied by camera C can be checked using the sign of `(Bx-Ax)*(Cy-Ay)-(By-Ay)*(Cx-Ax)`. Tolerance must be tied to the declared scale. When axis endpoints coincide, the camera is near the axis, or data is insufficient, record uncertainty rather than forcing a side assignment.

This side check verifies only the relationship within the provided geometry. It does not prove correct eyelines, lack of occlusion, attractive composition, or successful video execution. Start and end points alone do not prove a curved path never crosses the axis; preserve that verification gap when the trajectory is incomplete.

Aesthetic quality, performance effect, and narrative strength cannot be decided by filled fields, a ratio, or a machine score. Internal comparison must return to what the audience can actually see, when they understand relationships, and what each choice gains or loses.

## 8. Compilation and reverse acceptance

The cinematography draft and any storyboard or prompt must describe the same plan. Expand geometric names into subject position, margins, depth, occlusion, camera side, path, and endpoint. Do not redirect during compilation.

Before delivery, use only the final prompt to reconstruct the start, middle, cut points, and end. Confirm the main action is readable, composition changes have a basis, the axis is applicable and continuous, side changes have an explicit mechanism, and unknowns are not presented as verified facts.

Written rules and textual review prove only that this layer contains an executable description. Actual generation still requires inspecting real images and continuous playback. If execution deviates from selected cinematography, return to the appropriate stage under this package's correction process; do not automatically treat the generated result as a new directing decision.
