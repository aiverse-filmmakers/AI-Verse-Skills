# 5.6.0 Cinematography and Bold Shot Design Engine

## 1. Purpose

This file prevents shot design from being restricted to safe establishing shots, tracking, arcs, and pullbacks. When the story permits, actively use a broad cinematography vocabulary. Boldness does not mandate complexity, showmanship, or impossible spaces. It means choosing the most expressive viewpoint, optics, blocking, movement, editing, or visual transformation from the full toolbox and writing an executable process.

Together with script interpretation, the production contract, and the delivery gate in this directory, this forms a complete, independent cinematography design capability.

## 2. How to treat examples

The user may mention a Spielberg shot, a camera move named “Xixikeli” in transliteration (the intended term is unverified), fisheye, push-rise-pan, or another specific name. By default, these are illustrative evidence that the user wants broader, more proactive and creative direction. They are not a fixed four-item list, whole-film style, scope limit, or automatic override of locked story, characters, space, and shots.

Only when the user explicitly says to use or lock that shot or apply it throughout the film does an example become a chosen requirement. If a name is ambiguous, do not silently replace it with another term. Work from the visible effect the user supplied or the current scene design; when necessary ask only one question that changes the plan.

## 3. Full shot search space

Before design, actively search the dimensions relevant to this scene. Not every dimension must be used in every scene.

### 3.1 Shot function and information order

- Establishing, master, coverage, reaction, insert, reveal, delayed reveal, subjective, observational, surveillance, over-the-shoulder, two-person relationships, and ensemble blocking.
- Reaction before spectacle, result before cause, detail before whole, concealment before reveal, or holding the answer until the end of the shot.
- Continuous long take, cut coverage, jump cut, ellipsis, parallel action, crosscutting, action match, sound bridge, and rhythmic interruption.

### 3.2 Blocking and camera relationships

- Fixed observation, leading, following, parallel, opposing, rear-side, orbiting, crossing positions, and subject handoff.
- A character approaches, yields, makes space, occludes, reveals, enters foreground, exits frame, or changes depth.
- Camera follows a person, refuses to follow, is drawn along by them, waits ahead, catches up behind, or passes the subject and looks back at the result.

### 3.3 Shot size, composition, and viewpoint

- Extreme long, long, full, medium, close, close-up, extreme close-up, and non-stepwise jumps between sizes.
- High, low, ground-level, top-down, upward, tilted horizon, voyeuristic, first-person, body-mounted, object, and reflection viewpoints.
- Symmetry, imbalance, negative space, frame within frame, oppressive foreground, deep-focus depth, occlusion, central penetration, and entry at frame edge.

### 3.4 Focal length and optics

- Ultra-wide, wide, normal, telephoto, super-telephoto, macro, and effects of focal-length changes on perspective, compression, speed, and spatial relationships.
- Fisheye, split focus, shallow depth, deep focus, focus pull, focus relay, dolly zoom, anamorphic widescreen, mirrors, refraction, prisms, soft focus, lens breathing, and controlled distortion.
- Optical choices must change the audience's sense of distance, volume, psychology, speed, or information hierarchy, not merely name a lens.

### 3.5 Camera-center displacement and orientation

- Advance, retreat, lateral and longitudinal tracking, rise and descent, crane, jib, dolly track, orbit, spiral, arc, diagonal, dive, rise toward a top view, pass-through, drone, FPV, Steadicam, and handheld.
- Pan, tilt, and roll describe orientation changes separately from camera-center displacement.
- Push, rise, pan, tilt, track, zoom, and blocking can combine into a compound shot, but each phase needs a new visible task.

### 3.6 Motion quality and speed curve

- Complete stillness, breathing-like micro-movement, easing in, constant speed, acceleration, deceleration, abrupt stop, rebound, weightlessness, heavy handheld, mechanical precision, floating, impact, and inertial delay.
- Motion blur, shutter quality, speed ramps, time compression, and extension serve action readability and emotional pressure, not conceal performance or physical causality.

### 3.7 Occlusion, transitions, and in-shot handoffs

- Bodies, doorposts, clothing, vehicles, smoke, fire, shadows, reflections, light flares, motion blur, and physical surfaces can create occlusion or hidden stitches.
- Action, direction, composition, sound, color, shape, focus, and objects can hand off to the next shot or space.
- Whip pans, match cuts, wipes, passes through doors, windows, or reflections, foreground crossings, and sound leads require explicit entry, exit, and continuity anchors.

### 3.8 Time, space, and AI/CG extensions

- Local freezing, slow speed, rewind, time offsets, cloning, split screen, kaleidoscope, infinite zoom, scale penetration, gravity reversal, deconstruction and reassembly, non-Euclidean connections, and continuous environmental transformation.
- These are only part of the complete toolbox, not the default answer for bold shots. Physically filmable camera positions, optics, blocking, and editing can be equally bold.
- For visual transformation, lock real subjects and world anchors first; describe trigger, scope, whether the camera is affected, stages, and endpoint consequences.

## 4. From broad search to one completed design

Combine internally in this order; do not show a mechanical table to the user:

```text
Story change and viewing task
→ Blocking and information order
→ Audience viewpoint and shot function
→ Shot size, composition, focal length, and optics
→ Camera-center path and orientation
→ Focus, occlusion, speed, and movement quality
→ Editing / transition / visual transformation
→ Ending composition and dramatic result
```

### 4.1 Start selection from visible evidence

Identify changes in the script worth seeing: evidence on a small object, a suppressed reaction, a reversal between foreground and background, spatial information revealed during action, or a pause caused by refusing action. Do not wait for the user to name a technique; use existing changes to choose viewing distance, viewpoint, and shot organization.

Test decisive detail readability: at this aspect ratio, framing, and viewing time, can the audience distinguish what changed, or only see a person moving? If not, design viable alternatives such as an insert or reaction close-up, extreme close observation, enlarged foreground, deep-focus shared frame, or focus handoff, then compare to the original composition. A close-up must specify the observed body part, frame proportion or boundary, plane of sharpness, appearance, and hold. “Emotional close-up” or “85 mm” alone does not prove legibility. If a wide shot already shows body and spatial relationships adequately, do not force a close-up.

### 4.2 Candidates must change how the scene is viewed

When an unlocked cinematography decision materially affects expression and comparison would help, consider structurally different candidates, including an option outside default establishing shot → medium dialogue → slow push on reaction. Starting from the scene's readable evidence, change information order, viewing distance, spatial projection, camera-character relationship, or editing structure. Do not reopen a scene-wide contest for one locked shot or a small, clearly scoped revision.

Mentally rehearse start, middle, and end of each. Compare when viewers see what, whether performers can complete actions, how the shot changes relationships, and where information or performance is sacrificed. If only movement names differ while relationships, order, and endpoints remain the same, redesign one candidate.

Use a substitution test: if replaced with an ordinary eye-level medium shot, what specific evidence, physical feeling, spatial relationship, or rhythm is lost? If a complex shot becomes fixed framing, what is gained or lost? Choose from visible consequences, not number of terms, novelty, or safety. If fixed framing wins, explain the effective framing boundary, blocking, negative space, or hold, not simply “no movement needed.” Keep comparisons internal; do not ask the user to fill in reasons.

Explain geometry for special viewpoints and optics. For a tilted frame distinguish camera roll around its optical axis from the scene actually tipping; specify horizon tilt direction and amount, whether fixed or changing, entry and exit, and stable spatial anchors. For telephoto, wide, macro, or special focus also specify distance, field of view, and focused object. These are optional languages, not one-to-one mappings from tension, power, or sadness; not selecting them is not a defect.

### 4.3 Complete cinematography before handling implementation limits

Choose cinematography from story, performance, space, and time, then check known execution conditions. Mark unknown platform capabilities unknown; do not use uncertainty to automatically cancel special positions, compound moves, close observation, or in-shot handoffs, and do not claim support either. For a verified limit, preserve the cinematography draft, propose split generation, real editing, previs, or an equivalent technique, and check user locks. Do not silently substitute an unauthorized shot.

Genre or asset input supplies style, appearance, and scene facts. Defaults such as one movement per segment, no movement during dialogue, fixed eight seconds, or a minimum number of shots cannot dictate cinematography for this scene. First inherit user locks, then apply selected cinematography. Separate reference advice from actual platform hard limits. Do not rewrite the story to bypass a genre constraint or read another skill's internal implementation.

### 4.4 Keep a compilable cinematography draft

Internally retain a minimal draft per shot: specific viewing subject and composition change; shot size and actual frame boundaries; camera direction, height, pitch, and roll; optics and focus plane; camera path phases; character, focus, and occlusion handoffs; speed and hold; cut point and endpoint. Complex design is needed only for dimensions actually chosen; mark unchanged parameters as held.

Also save scene director intent and one observable reason for the chosen method, for example “keep the invitation and the unoccupied seat in the same frame,” rather than only focal length and movement. With a project directory, persist and restore using `references/design-memory-protocol.md`. Choose package knowledge from the scene's design problem, then check framing and axis with `references/framing-and-axis.md`. Knowledge broadens options; genre or emotion keywords cannot directly dictate a fixed shot.

The draft is not a new delivery table. A storyboard and any prompt are readable expressions of the same chosen cinematography; do not change the shot design during compilation.

## 5. Translate names into observable behavior

Director names, movements, shot names, and industry terms in user input or references can be research entry points, but formal storyboards and prompts must expand them into:

- Camera location, relation to subjects, and movement.
- How the camera center changes orientation.
- Focal length or optics and their effect on perspective and information.
- Blocking and when it triggers camera changes.
- Focus, occlusion, sound, or action handoffs.
- What the audience newly sees or understands at the endpoint.

“Director-style shot,” “fisheye,” “push-rise-pan,” or “cinematic camera movement” alone without this process is an incomplete design.

## 6. Input and execution responsibilities

- Text supplies story facts, character goals, action causality, shot structure, and visible process.
- Character, scene, and prop reference images supply approved appearances and landmarks, not automatic camera paths.
- A camera-motion reference video supplies speed curve, physical quality, occlusion rhythm, and motion grammar only. Do not copy people, scenery, brands, dialogue, sound, or a whole shot.
- 3D whitebox or clay previs supplies spatial topology, scale, blocking, camera path, collision, and occlusion, not final art direction.
- First, last, or key frames specify composition and state at particular moments; they do not prove intermediate motion will work.

When inputs are insufficient, the execution route or generation units may change, but never invent platform support or let lower-priority references override user-locked facts.

## 7. Internal acceptance

1. Was story change understood before selecting shots, rather than applying terms first?
2. Were relevant cinematography dimensions explored rather than stopping at safe moves?
3. Were specific user examples kept illustrative rather than turned into a fixed list or upper limit?
4. Does the chosen viewpoint, optics, blocking, movement, edit, or transformation have a visible narrative role?
5. Were names expanded into camera, focal length, characters, focus, speed, occlusion, and endpoint processes?
6. Is there effective variation in stillness and movement, distance, speed, focus, viewpoint, or information order rather than continuous showmanship?
7. Are identity, space, axis, props, light, environment, and physical continuity preserved?
8. Does the package contain all runtime files needed for this cinematography design?
