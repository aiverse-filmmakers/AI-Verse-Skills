# Composition and Blocking

Status: RUNTIME KNOWLEDGE
Task: 3.2

This reference converts visual intent into camera placement, subject arrangement, frame balance, spatial layering, and readable story geometry.

Composition is not a bag of aesthetic rules. It is the spatial system that determines what the viewer notices, how the viewer relates to the subject, and how much context survives.

Use `references/visual-intent.md` first.

## 1. Core Principle

Composition should answer three questions:

```text
What matters most?
Where is the viewer standing?
What information must remain readable?
```

Do not default to rule-of-thirds, centered symmetry, shallow depth, foreground blur, or low angles merely because they are visually familiar.

## 2. Decision Order

Choose composition in this order:

1. identify primary visual priority;
2. identify required contextual information;
3. establish viewer relationship;
4. establish subject blocking and action;
5. choose camera position and height;
6. choose shot size;
7. choose frame balance and negative space;
8. decide foreground/midground/background layering;
9. decide gaze/lead room and motion space;
10. check perspective and geometry plausibility;
11. only then choose focal length to achieve the required field of view from the intended camera position.

Do not use focal length as a shortcut for camera position.

## 3. Shot Size as Story Information

### Extreme close-up

Use when a tiny visual detail carries the story.

Examples:

- eye reaction;
- fingertip touching a surface;
- product mechanism;
- condensation bead;
- material detail.

Avoid using it simply to make an image feel dramatic.

### Close-up

Prioritizes face, object, or detail while retaining minimal context.

Useful for:

- emotional reading;
- product detail;
- intimate action;
- visual emphasis.

### Medium close-up

Balances facial expression with upper-body gesture and some environment.

### Medium

Useful when hands, posture, interaction, or nearby environment matters.

### Three-quarter / medium-wide

Useful when body language, wardrobe, action, furniture, props, or immediate environment matters.

### Full-body

Useful when stance, silhouette, wardrobe, movement, or surface contact matters.

### Wide

Useful when environment and subject relationship both matter.

### Extreme wide

Useful for scale, isolation, geography, architecture, landscape, crowd context, or environment-led storytelling.

Do not assume wider means more cinematic.

## 4. Subject Placement

Placement should follow visual intent, not a fixed grid.

### Centered

Useful for:

- confrontation;
- symmetry;
- product authority;
- formal portraiture;
- stillness;
- graphic geometry;
- deadpan or controlled compositions.

Centered does not automatically mean static or boring.

### Off-center

Useful for:

- environmental context;
- gaze or motion direction;
- asymmetry;
- tension;
- visual dialogue with another frame element;
- negative space.

### Edge-biased placement

Can increase instability, isolation, observational feeling, or reveal more environment, but must still preserve readable anatomy and intentional cropping.

## 5. Headroom

Headroom should feel intentional and proportional to shot size.

Avoid:

- accidental dead space above heads;
- hair/hat clipping that looks like a generation error;
- constantly perfect textbook headroom;
- forcing every portrait into identical framing.

Intentional top cropping can work in close portraits, fashion, documentary, or energetic compositions when it supports the frame.

## 6. Lead Room and Gaze Room

When a subject looks or moves toward one side of frame, normally provide visual room in that direction if the story benefits from anticipation or environmental context.

Break this deliberately when:

- compression/pressure is desired;
- the subject feels trapped;
- the unseen space behind matters more;
- confrontation with the edge is intentional.

Do not automatically add lead room if it weakens the brief.

## 7. Visual Hierarchy

Frame hierarchy may be controlled through:

- scale;
- placement;
- contrast;
- brightness;
- color separation;
- focus;
- occlusion;
- directional lines;
- negative space;
- depth position;
- repetition versus singularity.

Do not rely exclusively on background blur to create hierarchy.

A subject can remain dominant through lighting, contrast, placement, and scale while the environment stays legible.

## 8. Foreground Layers

Foreground elements can create:

- depth;
- viewer presence;
- observational framing;
- scale;
- concealment;
- narrative context;
- natural imperfection.

Examples:

- shoulder;
- door frame;
- foliage;
- table edge;
- vehicle interior;
- reflection;
- passing person;
- architecture;
- object near lens.

Use only when physically plausible from the chosen camera position.

Do not insert random blurry foreground objects solely because they look cinematic.

## 9. Extreme Foreground Distortion

When the creative brief intentionally calls for an exaggerated foreground object, distinguish foreground scale exaggeration from global fisheye distortion.

For a close hand, shoe, prop, or object:

- place it physically much closer to camera than the subject;
- use a wide rectilinear field of view if appropriate;
- allow strong near/far scale exaggeration;
- keep architecture/background lines comparatively rectilinear unless lens behavior is explicitly distorted;
- do not add circular/fisheye curvature unless requested.

This preserves the visual logic of an ultra-wide rectilinear shot.

## 10. Midground and Background

Use depth layers to explain the scene rather than filling space.

### Midground may provide

- supporting characters;
- product use context;
- furniture;
- road activity;
- architectural transition;
- secondary action.

### Background may provide

- location identity;
- scale;
- atmosphere;
- social context;
- weather;
- light sources;
- story clues.

Background detail should not compete equally with the subject unless the shot is intentionally ensemble/environment-led.

## 11. Negative Space

Negative space is active composition, not empty failure.

Use it for:

- isolation;
- calm;
- anticipation;
- typography-safe areas in commercial work;
- destination scale;
- architecture;
- visual imbalance;
- off-screen implication.

Do not fill negative space with decorative AI clutter.

## 12. Symmetry and Asymmetry

### Symmetry

Can communicate:

- control;
- ritual;
- luxury;
- architecture;
- formality;
- artificial order;
- stillness.

### Asymmetry

Can communicate:

- natural observation;
- movement;
- tension;
- imbalance;
- openness;
- documentary spontaneity.

Neither is inherently more cinematic.

## 13. Camera Height

Camera height strongly affects viewer relationship.

### Eye level

Neutral, human, observational, conversational.

### Slightly below eye level

Can increase presence without becoming overtly heroic.

### Low

Can emphasize scale, foreground, power, architecture, vehicles, or child/ground-level perspective.

Use carefully with faces and products because camera distance may produce strong near/far exaggeration.

### High

Can reveal layout, vulnerability, choreography, tabletop relationships, or environmental information.

### Top-down / 90-degree overhead

Use when spatial arrangement itself is the subject.

For a true 90-degree face-down view:

- optical axis should be perpendicular to the ground plane;
- vertical surfaces should not appear as though seen from an oblique angle unless their geometry naturally reveals them;
- subject and object footprints should read spatially;
- avoid a merely high three-quarter angle masquerading as top-down.

## 14. Camera Angle and Orientation

### Frontal

Direct, readable, confrontational, formal, product-friendly.

### Three-quarter

Adds dimensionality and natural facial/object form.

### Profile

Useful for silhouette, gaze direction, movement, relational blocking.

### Rear

Useful for observation, destination, anticipation, anonymity, POV handoff.

### Over-the-shoulder

Defines a relationship between foreground observer and target.

A proper OTS should establish:

- whose shoulder/body is foreground;
- what the viewer is looking toward;
- where the eyeline/action is;
- how much foreground body is visible;
- whether the camera is high, eye-level, or low relative to the shoulder.

Do not treat OTS as simply adding a random blurred shoulder.

### POV

The camera occupies the participant's spatial position.

Foreground body parts may be visible if natural, but are not mandatory.

### Dutch angle

Use only when tilt has an explicit narrative or graphic purpose.

Do not use as a generic tension preset.

## 15. Blocking

Blocking describes where subjects and important objects exist in three-dimensional space.

For each major subject, reason about:

```text
position
orientation
pose/action
gaze
relationship to other subjects
relationship to surfaces
foreground/midground/background depth
occlusions
contact points
```

Do not design camera framing independently of blocking.

## 16. Multi-Subject Composition

With multiple subjects:

- identify primary and secondary attention;
- preserve believable interpersonal distance;
- maintain eyeline relationships;
- prevent accidental mergers of limbs/faces;
- use depth and overlap intentionally;
- ensure all important actions remain readable;
- do not force everyone onto one flat plane unless the scene calls for it.

## 17. Over-the-Shoulder Blocking

For an OTS:

```text
foreground subject = observer
background/midground subject = target
camera = physically near/behind observer
```

The foreground shoulder/head should:

- establish spatial ownership;
- not obscure the target's key action;
- have perspective consistent with its close distance;
- remain secondary unless the story says otherwise.

For a high-angle OTS, camera height and tilt may reveal hands, ground, table, sand, object, or action below while preserving the observer's shoulder/head as foreground context.

## 18. Environmental Composition

When environment is important:

- choose camera position to reveal its identifying geometry;
- preserve enough depth and field of view;
- avoid blur so extreme that context becomes meaningless;
- allow subject scale to become smaller if scale is part of the story;
- use natural foreground/background layering where available.

## 19. Product Composition

For products:

- preserve proportions;
- preserve branding and functional geometry;
- choose an angle that explains form;
- control occlusions;
- make support/contact surfaces believable;
- treat reflections as part of composition;
- use props/environment only if they reinforce use, scale, or story.

Do not distort the product to make the frame more dramatic unless explicitly requested.

## 20. Automotive Composition

For vehicles:

- protect recognizable body proportions;
- avoid camera positions that accidentally stretch near bodywork beyond intent;
- preserve wheel geometry and ground contact;
- use low angles purposefully, not universally;
- consider leading space in direction of travel;
- allow environment/reflections to describe speed, prestige, terrain, or context.

## 21. Architecture Composition

For architecture:

- distinguish deliberate convergence from accidental warped geometry;
- preserve verticals when architectural clarity is important;
- use human/vehicle scale when needed;
- choose foreground objects for depth only when they belong naturally;
- avoid random ultra-wide stretching at corners.

## 22. Cropping and Anatomy

Intentional crops should occur at visually coherent locations.

Avoid accidental-looking crops through:

- joints;
- fingertips;
- toes;
- critical product features;
- logos;
- eyes;
- interaction points.

This is not an absolute ban. Editorial/fashion framing may intentionally crop aggressively, but it should read as authored rather than generated by mistake.

## 23. Frozen-Moment Consistency

When generating multiple camera views of the same instant:

> The scene is frozen. Only the camera moves.

Lock:

- subject pose;
- facial expression;
- gaze;
- hand positions;
- object positions;
- clothing state;
- environmental action;
- lighting state;
- shadows;
- timecode/moment.

Only camera position, orientation, focal/framing choices, and physically resulting visibility/parallax may change.

Do not turn a multi-camera request into a sequence of different poses.

## 24. Continuity From a Reference Frame

When reference composition must be preserved:

Separate:

```text
LOCKED CONTENT GEOMETRY
subject positions
pose
gaze
props
scene
moment

VARIABLE CAMERA GEOMETRY
position
height
angle
field of view
occlusion
parallax
```

Changing camera position legitimately reveals/hides different surfaces. Preserve the underlying scene rather than forcing identical 2D silhouettes from impossible viewpoints.

## 25. Avoid Generic Composition Filler

Do not automatically request:

- perfect rule of thirds;
- leading lines;
- foreground bokeh;
- centered hero framing;
- symmetrical composition;
- dramatic low angle;
- shallow focus;
- negative space;
- dutch angle.

These are tools, not quality tokens.

## 26. Composition Validation

Before accepting a shot, check:

- is the primary subject immediately legible?
- is the action readable?
- is viewer relationship correct?
- is required context present?
- is camera position physically plausible?
- does foreground occlusion make spatial sense?
- do headroom and lead room look intentional?
- are subject scale and environment scale coherent?
- are verticals/horizon appropriate to the brief?
- are important limbs/objects accidentally merged or cropped?
- did the frame become generic because a composition cliché was used automatically?
- are all explicit locks preserved?
